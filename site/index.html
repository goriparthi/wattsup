Charging demystified.

Live power telemetry for your Mac

Wattsup shows what your charger delivers, what your Mac uses and what your battery stores, right in the menu bar, and tells you when the charger or cable is holding you back.

Free and open source. macOS 14 or later on Apple silicon MacBooks. Notarized by Apple.

  • Notarized by AppleSigned with a Developer ID and checked by Gatekeeper.

The whole power path, one click away

Click the menu bar readout for a popover that shows where every watt is going. Charger, Mac and battery are joined by pipes whose thickness follows the watts, in the real direction of flow, the way a hybrid car shows its energy.

  • Power flow. Battery to Mac when unplugged, charger to Mac and battery when plugged in. Powered accessories branch off the Mac.
  • Battery level. A gauge whose surface moves while power flows, faster the more watts move in or out.
  • Charger check. A plain verdict, a five minute chart, then grouped details for charger, battery and Mac.
  • Reduce Motion aware. With it on, animations hold still and arrows show direction.

Know whether the charger or the cable is the bottleneck

Wattsup compares what your Mac would draw, its own load plus the charge power the battery is requesting, with what the charger's active USB-C Power Delivery profile can supply. After about 30 seconds on a charger it gives one of three verdicts.

Charger is the bottleneck
It runs at 90% or more of its limit while the Mac wants more. Wattsup suggests the smallest common size with 10% headroom.
Charger can't keep up
The battery drains while plugged in.
Charger has headroom
It keeps up, and may note that a lighter charger would also do.

When every profile stops at 3 A, Wattsup tells you to check the cable: a 100 W charger on a basic cable looks exactly like a 60 W one, and a 5 A cable unlocks the rest.

Charger report card

Every charger you use is graded from measured samples only. Each check stays ungraded until the load it needs has actually happened, and the overall grade is the weakest check.

  • Voltage under load

    How far the bus voltage sags as the charger works harder.

  • Cable resistance

    Derived from how voltage falls as current rises, which separates the cable from the charger.

  • Delivers its rating

    Whether it reached its limit when the Mac asked for more.

  • Connection

    Drop outs: brief disconnects while plugged in, often a worn cable or loose port.

  • Heat

    Whether macOS slowed charging because of heat.

A readout that fits how you work

The menu bar shows live numbers in an outlined capsule, colored by meaning: charger input blue, Mac use purple, charging green, draining pink. Hover any number for a tooltip that names it.

Choose from 16 readings, including power in or out, voltage, current, Mac uses, battery power, from the wall, charger loss, charger load, battery percent, time, temperature and health. Reorder them, stack them on two rows, or turn color and outline off.

Built for people who want the real numbers

The dashboard puts everything in one window, far beyond what the system battery settings show.

  • History up to 7 days

    Power, volts and amps, charge and temperature over 5, 15 or 60 minutes, 24 hours or 7 days. Minute averages are kept for 30 days.

  • Charger efficiency grade

    Delivered watts over wall watts, graded Excellent at 94% or more, then Good, Fair and Poor. Not rated under 10 W.

  • Power Delivery profiles

    Every USB-C profile the charger offers and which one is active, plus live input voltage and current.

  • Battery detail

    Capacity against design, cycle count, temperature, per cell voltages and imbalance.

  • Powering accessories

    USB devices the Mac is powering, such as an iPhone, with the power macOS allocated to each. These are ceilings, not live readings.

  • Energy mode control

    See and switch Low Power, Automatic or High Power, where the Mac offers it, separately for battery and charger.

  • Chargers you've used

    Every charger you plug in is remembered with its limit, peak load, quality grade and average efficiency.

  • Wall draw and loss

    Estimated power from the wall and the watts the charger turns into heat, from macOS's own loss estimate.

  • Replaces the battery icon

    Settings links straight to the macOS Menu Bar settings, so you can hide the built in battery icon.

Private by design, verifiable by anyone

Wattsup reads Apple's own battery and power telemetry on your machine, without admin rights. Your readings never leave your Mac, and the source is public so you can confirm it.

Updates are checked once a day against GitHub releases. An update installs only after you agree, and only after Wattsup verifies the download is notarized by Apple and signed by the same developer.

  • No data outReadings never leave the Mac. There is no account and no analytics.
  • One network requestA daily update check, on by default. Turn it off from the gear menu or Settings.
  • Verified updatesChecked by Gatekeeper and against the expected signing identity before install.
  • Local logsKept in ~/Library/Logs/Wattsup, yours to share only if something breaks.

Install in about two minutes

  1. Download the DMG from the latest GitHub release.
  2. Drag Wattsup into Applications and open it. It runs from the menu bar.
  3. Pick your readings in Settings, from the gear menu in the popover.

Frequently asked questions

Is Wattsup free?

Yes. It is free and open source under the MIT license, with no paid tier, account or trial.

Does it need admin rights?

Not to read anything. Readings come from the Mac's own battery and power registry, which any user can read. The only password prompt is when you change energy mode, because macOS only lets an administrator change power settings.

Why does it tell me to check the cable?

A USB-C charger only offers more than 3 A after it detects a 5 A cable, so a 100 W charger on a basic cable looks exactly like a 60 W charger. When every profile stops at 3 A and your charger is rated above 60 W, a 5 A cable rated 100 W or 240 W unlocks the rest. If a charger that reports its own name was seen delivering more on another cable, Wattsup says plainly that the cable is the bottleneck.

Does it send data anywhere?

No. Readings never leave your Mac. The only network request is a daily check of GitHub releases for an update, which you can turn off from the gear menu or Settings.

Which Macs are supported?

Apple silicon MacBooks running macOS 14 or later. High Power mode appears only on Macs that offer it.

How do updates work?

Once a day Wattsup checks GitHub releases. When a new version is available it asks first, then downloads it and installs it only if it passes Gatekeeper and is signed by the expected developer.

Are the accessory numbers live?

No. macOS reports the power it allocated to each USB device, so these are ceilings. Whatever a device actually draws is already part of Mac uses.
